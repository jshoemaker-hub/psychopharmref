Understanding Dementia

Dementia isn't one disease but a group of conditions that gradually affect memory, thinking, and behavior. Understanding it helps you respond with patience instead of frustration.

🧠

What Dementia Is

Dementia is a lasting decline in thinking abilities — memory, language, judgment — serious enough to affect daily life. It's caused by physical changes in the brain, not by choice, laziness, or stubbornness.

It is different from normal aging and from temporary confusion (delirium), which comes on fast and is often reversible.

🧩

Common Types

  • Alzheimer's — most common; memory usually first.
  • Vascular — from strokes or reduced blood flow; step-wise changes.
  • Lewy body — visual hallucinations, movement changes, alertness that fluctuates.
  • Frontotemporal — personality and behavior change early.
📈

How It Progresses

  • Early: forgetfulness, word-finding trouble, still largely independent.
  • Middle: more help needed with daily tasks; confusion and behavior changes grow.
  • Late: extensive help with all care; limited communication.

Progression varies widely from person to person.

💡

Why Behavior Changes

As the brain changes, so do emotions and behavior. Someone may repeat questions, become anxious at dusk, or act out of character.

These are symptoms of the illness, not deliberate. The person is usually trying to meet a need they can't express.

❤

What Still Remains

  • Feelings last long after facts fade — tone and warmth still land.
  • Long-term and emotional memories often persist.
  • Music, touch, and familiar routines remain comforting.
  • The person is still there — meet them where they are.
📋

Questions for the Clinician

  • What type of dementia is this, and what should we expect?
  • Which symptoms are treatable?
  • What can we do now to plan ahead?
  • Are there reversible contributors (medications, thyroid, B12, sleep)?
A sudden change is not "just the dementia." New confusion, agitation, or drowsiness over hours to days may mean infection, pain, dehydration, or a medication problem — call the clinician. These are often reversible.
