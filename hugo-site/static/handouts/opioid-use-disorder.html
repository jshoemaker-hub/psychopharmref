Opioid Use Disorder

Opioid use disorder is a treatable medical condition. Effective medications exist, recovery is common, and knowing how to prevent an overdose can save a life — yours or someone else's.

🩺

What It Is

Opioid use disorder (OUD) is a chronic condition in which the brain and body come to depend on opioids. Tolerance and withdrawal are physical signs — not moral failings.

Like other chronic illnesses, it responds well to ongoing treatment.

🚨

Overdose & Naloxone

Naloxone (Narcan) reverses an overdose in minutes. Keep it on hand and teach people around you.

Signs of overdose: can't wake up, slow or stopped breathing, blue lips or fingertips, gurgling.

  • Give naloxone, call 911, and start rescue breaths.
  • Repeat naloxone every 2–3 min if no response.
💊

Medications Save Lives

Medications for OUD cut the risk of overdose death by about half:

  • Buprenorphine (Suboxone) — eases cravings and withdrawal.
  • Methadone — daily dosing through a clinic.
  • Naltrexone (Vivitrol) — blocks opioid effects.

This is treatment, not "trading one drug for another."

🌬

Withdrawal

Withdrawal feels like a bad flu — aches, chills, nausea, restlessness, and cravings. It's miserable but not usually dangerous.

Medication can prevent most of it. Getting through withdrawal alone is not the same as treatment — the cravings return.

🛡

Stay Safer

  • Never use alone — have someone who can respond.
  • Assume fentanyl is present; test strips can help.
  • Don't mix opioids with alcohol or benzodiazepines.
  • Your tolerance drops fast after any break — a former dose can be deadly.
🤝

Getting Started

  • Ask any clinician about starting buprenorphine — many can prescribe it.
  • SAMHSA National Helpline: 1-800-662-4357 (free, confidential, 24/7).
  • Counseling and peer support strengthen recovery.
Naloxone belongs in your pocket, not a cabinet. Carry it, keep it current, and make sure the people you're with know where it is and how to use it. An overdose is survivable when someone acts fast.
