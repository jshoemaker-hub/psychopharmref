CIDI 3.0 Bipolar Screen: Symptom Count and Risk

Work through the CIDI 3.0 bipolar screen: two stem questions, a Criterion B gate, and nine symptoms mapped to a risk band. Copy a dated report.

Open the CIDI Bipolar Screen

Runs free in your browser. Scores are calculated on your device and not sent to a server.

What it measures

The CIDI 3.0 Bipolar Screening Scale is a brief screen for bipolar disorder in primary care and general clinical settings. It asks about past periods of elevated or irritable mood and the symptoms that came with them, and estimates how likely bipolar disorder is.

How the screen works

  • Stem questions. Q1 asks about a period of several days or longer of feeling much more excited and full of energy than usual. Q2 asks about a period of several days or longer of irritability bad enough to cause arguments, shouting or hitting. If both are "no", the screen is negative.
  • Criterion B gate. If either stem is "yes", Q3 asks whether any associated changes happened during those episodes. If Q3 is "no", the screen is negative.
  • Symptom count. If the gate is "yes", nine symptoms are checked: irritability with shouting or fights, restlessness or pacing, unusual or embarrassing behavior, grandiosity, increased activity or new projects, poor concentration, racing thoughts, decreased need for sleep, and overspending that caused financial trouble.

Interpreting the result

The number of symptoms endorsed (0-9) maps to a risk level and an estimated probability of bipolar disorder:

SymptomsRisk levelProbability
0-4Very low<5%
5Low5-24%
6Moderate25-49%
7-8High50-79%
9Very high≥80%

The tool cites Kessler RC et al. (J Affect Disord, 2006) for the validation and Gill JM et al. (J Am Board Fam Med, 2012) for the risk table.

What the report includes and its limits

The report lists the date, the stem and gate answers, the screen result with its risk level and probability, the symptoms endorsed, a clinical interpretation, the scoring note and the citations. A positive screen does not establish a diagnosis. It should lead to a full psychiatric evaluation, including longitudinal course, substance use, medical causes of mood elevation such as hyperthyroidism or stimulant use, and a safety assessment.
