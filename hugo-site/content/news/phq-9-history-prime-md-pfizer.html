Key points

  • The PHQ-9 grew out of PRIME-MD, a primary care diagnostic tool built in the early 1990s by Robert Spitzer, Janet Williams, Kurt Kroenke and colleagues with a Pfizer educational grant.
  • The 2001 validation in 6,000 primary care and obstetrics-gynecology patients found that a score of 10 or more had 88 percent sensitivity and 88 percent specificity for major depression.
  • Pfizer made the PHQ free to reproduce in 2010. It now anchors depression screening and measurement-based care, but overcounts depression when used as a diagnosis.

Few clinical forms are as familiar as this one: nine symptoms, a two-week window, and four answers per row, from "not at all" to "nearly every day." The PHQ-9 is filled out in waiting rooms and patient portals. It began as an attempt to get primary care physicians to notice mental illness, paid for by a drug company that had just entered the antidepressant market.

PRIME-MD: psychiatry for the 15-minute visit

Many patients with depression and anxiety are seen only by their primary care physician, and many are never recognized. In the early 1990s Robert Spitzer, who had chaired the DSM-III task force, and his long-time collaborator Janet Williams teamed up with primary care physician Kurt Kroenke and other colleagues to build a diagnostic tool for that setting. The result was PRIME-MD (Primary Care Evaluation of Mental Disorders), a rapid procedure that let a primary care physician make DSM diagnoses of common mental disorders during an ordinary visit.

The PRIME-MD 1000 study, published in JAMA in December 1994, tested it with 31 primary care physicians and 1,000 adult patients in four clinics. A quarter of patients (26 percent) met full DSM-III-R criteria for a disorder. Agreement with independent mental health professionals was good (kappa 0.71), and the physician's part took an average of 8.4 minutes. Nearly half of diagnosed patients known to their doctors had been missed.

From interview to self-report

Eight minutes was still too long. The team built a fully self-administered version, the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ). In a 1999 JAMA study of 3,000 patients seen by 62 physicians in eight clinics, PHQ diagnoses agreed with mental health professionals about as well as the original, and physicians needed less than three minutes to review it in 85 percent of cases.

The depression module stood on its own. In 2001 Kroenke, Spitzer and Williams published "The PHQ-9: validity of a brief depression severity measure" in the Journal of General Internal Medicine. Each of the nine DSM-IV criteria for major depression is scored 0 to 3, for a total of 0 to 27. Among 6,000 patients in eight primary care and seven obstetrics-gynecology clinics, with 580 re-interviewed by a mental health professional, a score of 10 or more had 88 percent sensitivity and 88 percent specificity for major depression. Cut points of 5, 10, 15 and 20 marked mild, moderate, moderately severe and severe depression. A two-item version, the PHQ-2, followed in 2003.

The Pfizer question

The printed forms said they were developed with an educational grant from Pfizer. In 2023 a STAT investigation reported that the idea had been pitched by a marketing executive, Howard Kroplick, at a time when Pfizer had recently launched the antidepressant Zoloft (sertraline) and was looking for ways to encourage primary care doctors to prescribe it. The reporting does not change the validation data, but the push to screen more widely had a commercial sponsor.

In July 2010 Pfizer announced that the PHQ and the anxiety scale GAD-7 could be downloaded, reproduced, translated and distributed without permission or charge, from a website offering them in nearly 80 languages. The company's chief medical officer, Freda Lewis-Hall, said: "By providing unrestricted access, Pfizer is encouraging broader usage of these important patient assessment aids."

Why it spread

Free, short and mapped onto DSM criteria, the PHQ-9 had few rivals. The US Preventive Services Task Force's 2016 recommendation to screen adults for depression added demand for a quick instrument. Measurement-based care, which uses repeated symptom scores to guide treatment, needed one too: a 2017 review by Fortney and colleagues found that frequent symptom feedback during visits improved outcomes, while one-time screening did not. From January 2018 the Joint Commission required accredited behavioral health organizations to monitor outcomes with a standardized instrument, and for depression the PHQ-9 was the obvious candidate.

The limits

  • It is a screen, not a diagnosis. In a 2020 individual-participant meta-analysis of 44 studies (9,242 participants), the share of patients scoring 10 or more was 24.6 percent, about twice the 12.1 percent who met interview criteria for major depression.
  • Accuracy depends on the comparison. A 2019 BMJ meta-analysis found sensitivity 0.88 and specificity 0.85 at a cut-off of 10 against semi-structured interviews, a little below the original figures.
  • It is not specific. Fatigue, poor sleep and appetite change also occur in medical illness, grief and substance use.
  • Item 9 is not a suicide risk assessment. A positive answer calls for a direct clinical conversation, not a score.

Clinical angle

The PHQ-9 is most useful for tracking change in a patient whose diagnosis has been confirmed by interview. A score of 10 or more is a reason to ask more questions, and a drop of 50 percent or more is a common working definition of response.

The PHQ-9 is an unexpected part of Spitzer's legacy: a nine-line form that carried DSM criteria into general medicine, blind spots included.
