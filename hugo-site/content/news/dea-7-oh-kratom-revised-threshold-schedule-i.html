Key points

  • On October 14, 2026, the DEA withdrew its July notice on 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) and published a new one, based on an October 8 recommendation from HHS.
  • The new plan covers 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl together, above a threshold of 1 mg per gram (solids) or 1 mg per mL (liquids), or when mitragynine is less than 100 times the amount of those compounds.
  • The temporary Schedule I order can be published on or after November 13. It takes effect on publication and can last up to three years.

The federal effort to control 7-hydroxymitragynine, the concentrated kratom-derived opioid sold in gas stations and smoke shops, changed course last month. Instead of finalizing the plan it announced in July, the Drug Enforcement Administration withdrew it and published a new notice of intent with a different threshold. Here is what changed, and what it means for clinicians who see patients using these products.

How we got here

On July 1, 2026, the DEA announced that it intended to temporarily place 7-OH and three related compounds in Schedule I. Notices appeared in the Federal Register on July 6. The 7-OH notice used a threshold HHS had recommended in July 2025: 0.05% 7-OH by dry weight for the kratom plant, and 0.05% or 1 mg per article for extracts, concentrates, edibles, pills and synthetic products. Kratom leaf below that level was not meant to be covered.

The three related compounds moved first. On August 26, the DEA placed mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15 and MGM-16 in Schedule I with no threshold, effective until August 26, 2028. The same day, it extended the comment period on 7-OH to September 10.

What changed in October

On October 8, the HHS Assistant Secretary for Health sent the DEA a new recommendation. On October 14, the DEA withdrew the July 7-OH notice and published a new notice of intent (Docket DEA-1570) built on that letter. It covers 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, alone or combined, when a product is either:

  • above 1 mg per gram for powders, capsules and other solids, or above 1 mg per mL for liquids; or
  • a product in which the mass of mitragynine, kratom's main alkaloid, is less than 100 times the mass of 7-OH and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl.

The second test targets the chemistry of the products, not just their strength. In the leaf, 7-OH is under 2% of total alkaloid content, according to the notice. It can be made from mitragynine in a single chemical step, and enriched products shift that balance. The notice says plainly that the new threshold would let some products stay on the market. It also says neither HHS nor the DEA has found these substances safe below the threshold.

The temporary order "will be published in the Federal Register on or after" 30 days from October 14. That makes Friday, November 13 the earliest date. It takes effect on publication and lasts two years, with a possible one-year extension if permanent scheduling is under way. The August order on mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, which has no threshold, stays in force until the new order replaces it. Federal control would not override stricter state kratom laws. As of early November, no 7-OH order had been published.

The evidence the DEA cites

The notice describes 7-OH as a mu-opioid agonist with a profile similar to Schedule II opioids such as morphine, including tolerance, dependence and respiratory depression. It is sold as powders, tablets, gummies and sublingual films, often labeled as natural kratom extract. The DEA lists several data sources:

SourceWhat the notice reports
U.S. poison centers165 exposures involving 7-OH from January to July 2025; of single-substance cases, 35% had serious outcomes and 67% were treated at a healthcare facility
FDA adverse event database (FAERS)86 cases as of February 27, 2026; 79 serious, including 9 deaths
DEA toxicology program7-OH found in 85 overdose cases since 2019, 55 of them fatal, often alongside fentanyl, bromazolam or ketamine

HHS reported that there are no investigational or approved new drug applications for 7-OH. Without one, it has no accepted medical use in the United States.

Clinical angle

Ask about kratom and 7-OH products directly. Patients often see them as herbal and may not mention them. Daily users of concentrated 7-OH can develop opioid-type dependence and withdrawal, and a sudden change in supply after scheduling may bring some of them to clinics or emergency departments. Screen for opioid use disorder and offer evidence-based treatment where it applies, as reviewed in our opioid use disorder chapter. Overdoses often involve other sedatives or opioids, so naloxone counseling is worth repeating. Background on kratom pharmacology is in Legal and Atypical Drugs of Abuse.

What to watch

Watch the Federal Register from November 13 for the temporary order. Its text will set the final threshold and say how the DEA handles the overlap with the August order. Bills in Congress, including the END 7-OH Act (H.R. 8000), would address 7-OH by statute, but none had become law by early October, according to the Congressional Research Service.
