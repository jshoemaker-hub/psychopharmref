Key points

  • From January 1, 2027, adults aged 19 to 64 in Medicaid's expansion group must show 80 hours a month of work, school, job training, volunteering or similar activity, unless they are exempt. The requirement comes from the 2025 reconciliation law, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act.
  • CMS's interim final rule, published June 3, 2026, says a substance use disorder or a "disabling" mental disorder is not enough for the medically frail exemption. The condition must also significantly impair the person's ability to meet the requirement, and a diagnosis code alone cannot establish this.
  • If a state cannot confirm an exemption from its own data, a clinician's documentation can establish it. Psychiatric notes are about to become eligibility evidence.

In eight weeks, Medicaid coverage for millions of low-income adults will depend on proving that they work, study or volunteer. People with serious mental illness and substance use disorders are among those least able to handle the reporting. The federal rule decides how easily they can claim an exemption, and in key respects it is stricter than the statute's wording suggested.

What is final, and what could still change

CMS issued the rule (CMS-2454-IFC) as an interim final rule with comment period, published in the Federal Register on June 3, 2026. That means it is binding now, though CMS asked for comments, which closed July 31, and may revise it in a later final rule. States that cover the expansion adult group must start the requirement no later than January 1, 2027. Nebraska started early, on May 1, 2026.

The statute lets CMS give a state more time if it shows a "good faith effort," but not beyond December 31, 2028. The interim rule grants these exemptions only six months at a time, with quarterly progress reports required. In October, governors of several states, including California, Oregon, Maine, New Mexico and Virginia, asked HHS for a delay. As of this writing we found no public CMS approval of any state's request. Clinicians should assume their state will start on time unless it announces otherwise.

How the rule treats psychiatric illness

The statute lists exemptions, among them people participating in a drug or alcohol treatment or rehabilitation program. It also exempts the "medically frail," a group defined in five categories: people who are blind or disabled; people with a physical, intellectual or developmental disability that significantly limits activities of daily living; people with a substance use disorder; people with a disabling mental disorder; and people with a serious or complex medical condition.

The rule adds a requirement on top of that list. According to KFF's analysis, CMS reads the exemption to require both a qualifying condition and evidence that the condition significantly impairs the person's ability to complete 80 hours of activity. States may not exempt anyone on diagnosis alone and may not create their own exemption categories. Someone who is already meeting the hours requirement cannot be counted as medically frail.

For substance use disorder, ASAM's reading of the rule is that the SUD category covers people in the first five years of recovery. Being in recovery for five or more years does not qualify a person on the basis of SUD alone. Being in active treatment is a separate route to exemption.

In its July 21 comment letter, the American Psychiatric Association argued that the "significantly impairs" test has no basis in the statute and defeats the purpose of the exemption. The APA asked CMS to remove it before finalizing the rule. CMS has not yet responded in a final rule.

Why the chart matters

States must check exemptions against data they already hold, such as claims and diagnosis codes, before asking enrollees for anything. If that check fails, practitioners can submit documentation of eligibility. In practice, a psychiatrist's note describing how a patient's illness limits sustained work or attendance may be what keeps that patient covered.

Clinical angle

For patients on long-acting injectables, clozapine monitoring or buprenorphine, a lapse in coverage can quickly become a lapse in treatment. Identifying at-risk patients before January matters more than any single form.

What to do now

  1. Find out your state's process. Ask your state Medicaid agency or managed care plans how medically frail status is verified and what clinician forms, if any, they will accept.
  2. Document function, not just diagnosis. For patients in the expansion group, record specifically how symptoms affect their ability to keep a schedule, sustain work or attend training.
  3. Note treatment participation. For patients with SUD, clearly document enrollment in a treatment program and recovery status.
  4. Prepare staff and patients for documentation requests and mail from the state.

The interim rule could change if CMS issues a final version. The January 1 date can be moved only by a CMS-approved good-faith exemption or by Congress.
