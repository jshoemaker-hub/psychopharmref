Key points

  • The federal Departments of Labor, HHS and the Treasury have told a federal court they will not defend the 2024 Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act (MHPAEA) rule. They plan to propose a replacement with "significant revisions," and intend to send it to the White House Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs (OIRA) by December 18, 2026.
  • The parts of the 2024 rule that were new are not being enforced. The parity statute, the 2013 regulations and the 2021 requirement for comparative analyses still apply.
  • A September 8 Labor Department bulletin narrows enforcement to three areas: blanket treatment exclusions, medical necessity review (prior authorization, concurrent and retrospective review), and network adequacy, including how plans set provider payment.

Two years after it was finalized, the most detailed federal rule on mental health parity is still on the books, still unenforced, and about to be rewritten. For clinicians who fight insurance denials, what matters is what still has teeth while the rule is redone.

How the 2024 rule stalled

The Departments issued the rule on September 9, 2024 (89 FR 77586). It took effect November 22, 2024, with most new requirements applying to plan years beginning on or after January 1, 2026. Among other things, it added a "meaningful benefits" standard. It also barred plans from using discriminatory factors and evidentiary standards when designing nonquantitative treatment limitations (NQTLs), such as prior authorization, and required plans to collect outcome data, like denial rates and network participation, to test whether those limits work out differently for mental health.

On January 17, 2025, the ERISA Industry Committee (ERIC), a trade group for large employers that sponsor health plans, sued in federal district court in Washington. The case has been on hold since May 2025. That month, the Departments announced they would not enforce the provisions that were new compared with the 2013 rule. Nonenforcement lasts until a final decision in the litigation, plus 18 months. HHS encouraged state insurance regulators, who enforce parity for insurers, to do the same.

A replacement, on a timeline the plaintiff disputes

In a March 30, 2026 filing, the Departments said they intended to propose a new rule no later than December 31, 2026. In a September 30 joint status report, they went further. They said they would not defend the 2024 rule and would instead propose one with significant revisions to the provisions ERIC challenged. But they now commit only to sending the draft to OIRA on or before December 18. OIRA review ordinarily takes up to 90 days, so a public proposal could come well into 2027.

ERIC called that a dramatic change from earlier promises. It noted that four Labor Department draft rules sent to OIRA in the past two years were withdrawn without ever being proposed. ERIC said the government should have to answer the lawsuit by January 15, 2027 if no proposal appears by December 31. According to the Georgetown litigation tracker, the court's October 5 scheduling order set the next joint status report for November 16, 2026. Nobody outside government has seen the draft, so it is not known which 2024 provisions will survive.

What Labor will actually enforce

Field Assistance Bulletin 2026-03, issued September 8 by the Employee Benefits Security Administration (EBSA), tells investigators to focus requests for NQTL comparative analyses on three categories it describes as having the highest potential for harm:

  1. Separate treatment limitations, including exclusions. Plans generally cannot exclude a treatment for a covered mental health or substance use condition when similar treatment is covered for medical conditions. EBSA will concentrate on blanket exclusions but may pursue narrower ones, especially after complaints.
  2. Medical necessity standards and review. These include prior authorization, concurrent review and retrospective review. Plans may use proprietary clinical guidelines. They must apply them no more strictly than on the medical side and produce the criteria on request.
  3. Network adequacy. The focus is on network admission standards and how plans set provider reimbursement. When no in-network clinician is available for a covered service, EBSA says it will make sure plans help members get care without paying out-of-network costs.

The bulletin is internal policy that creates no new rights, and EBSA says it may still look at other limits when complaints arrive. It also applies only to employer-sponsored plans under ERISA. State regulators set their own priorities for fully insured products.

In Congress, the Parity Enforcement Act of 2025 (H.R. 957), introduced by Rep. Donald Norcross with bipartisan cosponsors, would let the Labor Department impose civil monetary penalties for parity violations. It remains in the House Education and Workforce Committee.

Clinical angle

The tools that are still enforced line up with the most common problems in psychiatric practice: inpatient and residential stays cut short by concurrent review, prior authorization on mental health services that comparable medical services don't need, and networks with no available psychiatrists. When appealing, ask the plan in writing for the medical necessity criteria it used. Federal law requires plans to provide them. For employer plans, patients can also ask the plan for its NQTL comparative analysis or contact EBSA. A complaint in one of the three priority areas is the kind EBSA has said it will act on.
