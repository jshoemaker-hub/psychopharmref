Key points

  • The first Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (1952) was adapted from the Army's wartime nomenclature, Medical 203, which in turn replaced a hospital statistics manual first issued in 1918.
  • DSM-I described most conditions as "reactions," reflecting Adolf Meyer's psychobiological view that mental disorders are responses of the whole person to psychological, social and biological pressures.
  • DSM-II (1968) dropped "reaction." DSM-III (1980) replaced short prose descriptions with explicit diagnostic criteria and a stance neutral on causes.

The first DSM was a slim book of short definitions with limited bearing on everyday practice. Seventy-four years on, its title is shared by one of the most influential books in medicine. How it came to exist, and why its successors rejected so much of it, is a story about who psychiatrists were treating and what they thought mental illness was.

Counting the insane

American psychiatric classification began as statistics, not diagnosis. According to the American Psychiatric Association, the 1840 census recorded a single category, "idiocy/insanity," and the 1880 census distinguished seven: mania, melancholia, monomania, paresis, dementia, dipsomania and epilepsy.

In 1918 the American Medico-Psychological Association (renamed the American Psychiatric Association in 1921) and the National Committee for Mental Hygiene, working with the Bureau of the Census, published the Statistical Manual for the Use of Institutions for the Insane. Historian Gerald Grob called it the first official American nosology and noted that it existed mainly because the Census Bureau needed uniform data from mental hospitals. Its 22 categories were mostly psychoses with a presumed bodily cause, which suited asylum populations.

The war changes the patients

The Second World War brought psychiatrists out of the asylums and into induction centres and field hospitals, where most of the problems they saw were not psychoses. The existing classifications lacked categories for many of the conditions that troubled soldiers. The Army's response was a new classification issued by the Office of the Surgeon General as War Department Technical Bulletin, Medical 203, prepared by a committee chaired by William C. Menninger. Psychologist Arthur Houts, who dates the bulletin to 1943, describes it as the first psychodynamic nomenclature. A version was reprinted for civilian use in 1946 as Nomenclature of Psychiatric Disorders and Reactions.

Returning military psychiatrists brought Medical 203 into civilian clinics, while hospitals still used the older system and the Veterans Administration adapted its own. The APA's Committee on Nomenclature and Statistics was asked to replace this patchwork. Its manual, published in 1952, followed Medical 203 closely in structure, and Houts traces how much of the text carried over. The APA describes DSM-I as a variant of the World Health Organization's ICD-6, and the first official manual of mental disorders to focus on clinical use, with a glossary describing each category.

Everything was a reaction

DSM-I listed roughly a hundred diagnoses (Kawa and Giordano count 102; other tallies differ depending on how subtypes are counted). They were sorted into disorders caused by, or associated with, impaired brain function and disorders without a clearly defined physical cause. Within the second group were psychotic disorders, psychoneurotic disorders and personality disorders. Many names shared one word: schizophrenic reaction, manic-depressive reaction, anxiety reaction, depressive reaction.

The APA attributes that word to Adolf Meyer, the Swiss-born psychiatrist who directed the Phipps Clinic at Johns Hopkins and taught that mental disorders were reactions of the personality to psychological, social and biological factors. In this view illness was a pattern of maladaptation in a particular life, not a fixed disease entity.

DSM-I is often called a psychoanalytic manual. Rachel Cooper and Roger Blashfield challenged that label in 2016: psychoanalysts were a minority on the committee and did not use it much, and its categories owed as much to Emil Kraepelin as to Freud. They also point out that it was popular, going through 20 printings.

The break: DSM-II and DSM-III

DSM-II, published in 1968 alongside the closely aligned ICD-8, removed "reaction." Its committee wrote that it had tried to avoid terms implying a disorder's nature or cause. The manual still gave short prose descriptions, and in practice clinicians often disagreed about which one fitted a patient. In 1974 Robert Spitzer and Joseph Fleiss reanalysed reliability studies and found agreement satisfactory for only a few broad categories.

Spitzer chaired the task force for the next edition, which began work in 1974. Drawing on the Feighner criteria (1972) and the Research Diagnostic Criteria, DSM-III appeared in 1980 with explicit diagnostic criteria for each disorder, a multiaxial assessment, and a deliberately neutral position on causes. Historian Mitchell Wilson described the change as a shift from a clinically based biopsychosocial model to a research-based medical model, one that improved reliability but narrowed psychiatry's clinical gaze.

Clinical angle

Old charts and older literature use DSM-I and DSM-II terms such as "depressive reaction" or "anxiety neurosis." They do not map neatly onto current diagnoses, so read them as descriptions of the clinical picture, not as equivalents of modern criteria-based categories.

DSM-I's idea of illness as a response to a life still survives in the way clinicians take a history, even though the manual's word for it is gone.
