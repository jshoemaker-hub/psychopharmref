Key points

  • Aaron T. Beck (1921–2021) trained as a psychoanalyst. In the late 1950s he set out to test the Freudian idea that depression is anger turned inward, and his dream studies pointed him somewhere else: toward themes of loss, defeat and inadequacy.
  • The Beck Depression Inventory (1961) and the "cognitive triad" of negative views of the self, the world and the future came out of that turn. In 1977 his group reported that cognitive therapy outperformed imipramine in depressed outpatients.
  • His later work measured hopelessness as a predictor of suicide, tested a brief cognitive therapy for people who had attempted suicide, and adapted the approach for low-functioning patients with schizophrenia. He died in Philadelphia on 1 November 2021, aged 100.

Aaron T. Beck was born on 18 July 1921, the son of Russian immigrants who settled in Providence, Rhode Island. He trained first in neurology, spent six months in psychiatry during his residency and moved into the field. By 1956 he had finished training as a Freudian psychoanalyst. At the University of Pennsylvania, where he worked for nearly seven decades, he ended up taking apart the theory he had set out to prove.

Testing Freud with dreams

Psychoanalytic theory in the 1950s held that depression was hostility turned against the self. If that were true, Beck reasoned, depressed patients' dreams should be full of disguised anger. His first paper on the question, written with M. S. Hurvich and published in Psychosomatic Medicine in 1959, still used the vocabulary of the day: it counted "masochistic" dream content in a private-practice sample. A 1961 paper with C. H. Ward described the characteristic themes in depressed patients' dreams.

What the dreams showed, as the Lasker Foundation later summarized it, was not anger but loss and personal inadequacy: the dreamer rejected, thwarted or defeated. Beck saw the same pattern in his office. A patient worried that her stories were boring him; when he asked other patients what was running through their minds, he heard similar quick, negative judgments about themselves. He called them "automatic thoughts." By 1961, by the Lasker account, he had given up psychoanalysis.

An inventory and a triad

To study depression he needed a way to measure it. In June 1961 Beck, Ward, Mendelson, Mock and Erbaugh published "An inventory for measuring depression" in the Archives of General Psychiatry. Its 21 items asked about symptoms and attitudes, each graded by severity. The Beck Depression Inventory became one of the most widely used depression measures in the world; a revised version, the BDI-II, appeared in 1996.

His 1963 paper "Thinking and depression" described the idiosyncratic content and systematic distortions in depressed patients' thinking. In his 1967 book on depression he organized them into the cognitive triad: a negative view of the self, of the world and of the future. In treatment, therapist and patient would treat automatic thoughts as hypotheses and test them against evidence, usually over a short course of weekly sessions. The 1979 manual Cognitive Therapy of Depression (Beck, Rush, Shaw and Emery) made the method teachable.

Against imipramine

A resident, John Rush, told Beck that nobody would believe the new therapy without a clinical trial, as Beck recalled in his Lasker remarks. The trial Rush, Beck, Kovacs and Hollon published in 1977 randomly assigned 41 depressed outpatients, many chronically ill, to individual cognitive therapy (19) or imipramine (22), then the standard antidepressant. Cognitive therapy produced significantly greater improvement, and gains held at follow-up. It was a small study, and larger later trials, such as Hollon and colleagues' 1992 comparison, found the two roughly equivalent. Still, it showed that psychotherapy could be tested head to head with a drug.

Suicide and schizophrenia

Beck spent much of his later career on suicide. With colleagues he published the Hopelessness Scale in 1974. In a 1985 follow-up of 207 patients hospitalized for suicidal ideation, the Hopelessness Scale and the pessimism item of the BDI were the only baseline measures that predicted the 14 later suicides; a score of 10 or more identified 91% of them. In 2005 Gregory Brown, Beck and colleagues reported in JAMA that 10 sessions of cognitive therapy roughly halved repeat suicide attempts over 18 months in 120 people seen after an attempt.

In his last years he turned to people with serious mental illness. A 2012 trial led by Paul Grant randomized 60 low-functioning patients with schizophrenia to 18 months of recovery-oriented cognitive therapy plus usual care or usual care alone; the therapy group improved more in global functioning, avolition-apathy and positive symptoms. That work became Recovery-Oriented Cognitive Therapy (CT-R), his final project.

Clinical angle

Beck's tools are still in daily use: the BDI-II for tracking depressive symptoms, hopelessness as a warning sign in suicide risk formulation, and cognitive therapy as a first-line option alongside or instead of antidepressants for nonpsychotic depression. Legacy

Beck cofounded the Beck Institute for Cognitive Behavior Therapy with his daughter Judith Beck in 1994 and won the Albert Lasker Clinical Medical Research Award in 2006. When he died, his Penn colleague Martin Seligman told the Philadelphia Inquirer that Beck was "the single most important psychiatrist in the world of the 20th century." His career began as an attempt to confirm a theory; its lesson is that he let the data change his mind.
