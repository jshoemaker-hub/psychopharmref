Updated Nov 24, 2026: This piece first ran on November 11, ahead of the FDA's November 14 goal date. The FDA approved at-home use on November 13, and we have rewritten it to reflect the approval letter and the new label.

Key points

  • On November 13, 2026, the FDA approved a supplemental application (NDA 215390/S-003) that lets adults use the 120 mcg dose of Igalmi (sublingual dexmedetomidine) at home for acute agitation associated with schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, or bipolar I or II disorder. The same action added schizoaffective disorder to the indication.
  • At home the dose is a single 120 mcg film. Another dose may follow only after at least 12 hours, and no more than one dose may be taken within 12 hours. Unsupervised use is not recommended for patients 65 or older or with any degree of hepatic impairment.
  • The label's at-home safety data come from SERENITY At-Home, a 12-week placebo-controlled outpatient study in 208 adults. Teva, which bought Igalmi from BioXcel in late October, announced the approval on November 16.

Agitation in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder is treated almost entirely in emergency departments, inpatient units and crisis settings. On November 13, a day before its goal date, the FDA approved one of those treatments for use at home. The approval letter for Igalmi (dexmedetomidine sublingual film), signed by Tiffany Farchione, director of the FDA's Division of Psychiatry, expands the 120 mcg dose to outpatient settings and adds schizoaffective disorder to the indication. Teva announced the approval on November 16 and called Igalmi the first FDA-approved treatment that can be used at home for this kind of agitation.

What the new label allows

Igalmi was approved in 2022 for the acute treatment of agitation associated with schizophrenia or bipolar I or II disorder in adults, given under the supervision of a healthcare provider. It is a thin film placed under the tongue or between the lower lip and gum, and it delivers dexmedetomidine, an alpha-2 adrenergic agonist long used as an intravenous ICU sedative. The revised label now describes two settings:

  • Healthcare supervised: 120 mcg or 180 mcg by agitation severity, with lower doses for hepatic impairment and patients 65 and older.
  • Unsupervised (at home): 120 mcg only, once for an episode. If agitation recurs, another 120 mcg dose may be taken after at least 12 hours. No more than one dose within a 12-hour period.

The label does not recommend unsupervised use in patients 65 or older or in those with mild, moderate or severe hepatic impairment. At home, patients should avoid combining Igalmi with sedatives, hypnotics or opioids because of the risk of added CNS depression. After a dose they should drink fluids and sit or lie down until their vital signs are normal, and they should not drive or operate hazardous machinery for at least eight hours. Patients should also receive treatment for the underlying disorder, and if Igalmi is used frequently, the label says to reassess whether that treatment is adequate. Igalmi is not indicated to prevent agitation, and the safety and effectiveness of chronic, daily use have not been studied.

The safety data behind it

The at-home safety data come from SERENITY At-Home (Study 3 in the label), a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial built to test safety, not efficacy. Adults with a history of agitation episodes took a 120 mcg dose or placebo themselves when an episode began, over 12 weeks. The label reports 208 adults in the outpatient study (102 on Igalmi, 106 on placebo), 53% with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder and 47% with bipolar I or II disorder. Adverse reactions were reported within 24 hours of dosing:

  • Somnolence (including fatigue and sedation): 55% on Igalmi versus 42% on placebo.
  • Dry mouth: 15% versus 4%.
  • Dizziness: 11% versus 3%.
  • Oral paresthesia or hypoesthesia: 5% versus 2%.

Teva reports that 1,160 agitation episodes were treated with Igalmi in the trial's 102 active-arm patients, with no discontinuations for tolerability. Efficacy at home was not formally tested. The label's efficacy data still come from the original supervised trials, and the full SERENITY At-Home results have not been published in a peer-reviewed journal that we could find.

A change of owner mid-review

BioXcel Therapeutics, which developed Igalmi and submitted the supplement in January 2026, filed for Chapter 11 in the District of Delaware in late August, with Teva as the stalking-horse bidder for its neuroscience assets. The court approved the sale on October 21, and it closed on October 26. Teva paid $57.5 million in cash and agreed to pay up to $67.5 million more if the FDA approved at-home use, with the amount falling the later approval came. Teva has said it will announce commercialization plans for at-home Igalmi at a later date.

Clinical angle

At-home Igalmi is a rescue dose, not a maintenance drug. Before prescribing it, check heart rate and orthostatic blood pressure, screen for hypotension, heart block, severe ventricular dysfunction, syncope and QT risk, and confirm the patient is under 65 with normal liver function. Teach the patient and any caregiver the limits: one 120 mcg film, nothing more for 12 hours, no sedatives, hypnotics or opioids alongside it, sit or lie down afterward and no driving for eight hours. If doses become frequent, treat that as a sign the underlying treatment needs review. Other acute options are covered in our emergency psychiatry chapter, and QT considerations in Psychopharmacology in Long QT Syndrome.

What comes next

Teva has not yet said when the at-home film will be available or what it will cost. Under the approval letter, the company must also complete deferred pediatric studies, in patients 13 to 17 with schizophrenia and 10 to 17 with bipolar disorder, starting with a juvenile animal study.
