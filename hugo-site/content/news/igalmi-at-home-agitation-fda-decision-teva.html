Key points

  • The FDA's goal date for a supplemental application to use Igalmi (sublingual dexmedetomidine) at home for acute agitation in schizophrenia or bipolar disorder is Saturday, November 14, 2026.
  • The application rests on SERENITY At-Home, a 12-week safety trial of a 120 mcg dose taken by patients during agitation episodes, and not on a new efficacy trial.
  • BioXcel sold Igalmi to Teva in a Chapter 11 sale that closed in late October. Up to $67.5 million of the price depends on approval.

Agitation in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder is treated almost entirely in emergency departments, inpatient units and crisis settings. This week the FDA is due to decide whether one of those treatments can move into the home. The goal date for the at-home application for Igalmi (dexmedetomidine sublingual film) falls on Saturday, November 14. The agency can act on or before a goal date, so a decision may come earlier.

What Igalmi is approved for now

Igalmi was approved in 2022 for the acute treatment of agitation associated with schizophrenia or bipolar I or II disorder in adults. It is a thin film placed under the tongue or against the cheek, and it delivers dexmedetomidine, an alpha-2 adrenergic agonist long used as an intravenous ICU sedative. The current label says it should be given under the supervision of a healthcare provider, who should check vital signs and alertness afterward to prevent falls and syncope. The label also states that safety and effectiveness have not been established beyond 24 hours from the first dose.

The label's warnings cover hypotension, orthostatic hypotension and bradycardia, QT prolongation, somnolence, withdrawal reactions, and tolerance. In the original trials, somnolence was the most common adverse reaction, in roughly 22–23% of patients on Igalmi versus 6% on placebo.

What the company asked for

BioXcel Therapeutics submitted the supplemental application in January 2026. The FDA accepted it for at-home use in the acute treatment of agitation associated with bipolar disorders or schizophrenia, and set the November 14 goal date. If approved, BioXcel had said, it would be the first FDA-approved at-home option for this use.

The SERENITY At-Home trial

The supporting study was a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial built to test safety, not efficacy. Adults with a history of agitation episodes, living alone or with a caregiver, took a 120 mcg dose or placebo themselves when an episode began, over 12 weeks. Topline results reported by the company in August 2025:

  • 246 patients randomized; 2,437 agitation episodes treated in 208 patients, about 11.7 per patient.
  • Somnolence (including fatigue) in 13.9% of episodes treated with dexmedetomidine versus 8.1% with placebo. On the first dose, 22.5% versus 17%.
  • No drug-related serious adverse events, syncope or falls reported, and no discontinuations for tolerability in the active arm.

Efficacy was exploratory. The company said treatment regularly reduced agitation but did not report formal efficacy statistics in that announcement. The full data have not been published in a peer-reviewed journal that we could find, and the FDA's review documents will not be public until after a decision.

A change of owner mid-review

BioXcel filed for Chapter 11 in the District of Delaware in late August, with Teva as the stalking-horse bidder for its neuroscience assets. The court approved the sale on October 21, and it closed on October 26. Teva paid $57.5 million in cash. It owes up to $67.5 million more if the FDA approves at-home use, with the amount falling the later approval comes. If approval does not come by February 28, 2027, sales milestones apply instead. Teva has said it sees the product as a fit for its neuroscience portfolio, which already includes long-acting antipsychotics.

Clinical angle

Even if approved, at-home use raises practical questions the label will need to answer: who chooses to take a dose, how a caregiver judges blood pressure and alertness without monitoring, what to do with a second dose, and how to handle patients with bradycardia, orthostasis, QT risk or other sedatives on board. Wait for the final label before changing any crisis plan. For now, Igalmi remains a supervised treatment, alongside the options reviewed in our emergency psychiatry chapter. QT considerations are covered in Psychopharmacology in Long QT Syndrome.

What to watch

An approval would come with a revised label, and its wording on supervision, dose limits and who may give the film will matter more than the headline. A complete response letter would set out what the FDA still needs, and Teva would decide how to respond. We will report the outcome once it is public.
