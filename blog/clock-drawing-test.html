The Clock-Drawing Test: Patterns, Scoring, and What They Suggest

Instructions, the executive demand of 10 past 11, command versus copy, and schematic patterns across common clinical syndromes.

Published: October 12, 2026 For Clinicians & Medical Students 14 min read Jerad Shoemaker, MD

The clock-drawing test is a two-minute screen of whether someone can hold the idea of a clock, lay the numerals out in space, and set the hands to a time that has to be recoded. Medical students meet it inside the MoCA and the SLUMS. Clinicians use it at the bedside because the errors are visible. This chapter is a way to look at those errors: what the instruction is asking, why the usual time is 10 past 11, how command and copy pull on different systems, and what a few typical patterns suggest about the networks involved. The patterns are typical. They are not diagnoses.

Screening, then the rest of the examination. A clock can support a concern for cognitive impairment and can suggest where to look next. It does not establish Alzheimer disease, a stroke, or any other diagnosis. Education, literacy, vision, and motor control all change the drawing. Shulman’s review of studies published through 1998 found a mean sensitivity of 85% and a mean specificity of 85% across scoring systems — an average over heterogeneous methods, not the performance of one cutoff in your clinic.

How to give it

Give the person a blank sheet and a pen. Say the three parts separately, and wait for each one before you add the next. A common bedside script, the one used with Shulman’s scale and with the Mini-Cog, is:

1. “I’d like you to draw a clock.”

2. “Put in all the numbers.”

3. “Set the hands to 10 past 11.”

Some protocols name the time differently. Sunderland’s 10-point scale asked for 2:45. The time is part of the method. If you change it, you change which errors the task can provoke, and you should score with the system that matches the instruction you used.

Why 10 past 11

“Ten past eleven” names two numerals, 10 and 11, and then asks the person not to point at them. A correct clock puts the minute hand on the 2, because 10 minutes is 10/60 of the hour-circle, and it puts the shorter hour hand a short way past the 11, toward the 12 — about one-sixth of the distance from 11 to 12. That recoding is an executive step. The frontal pull is toward the numbers just spoken. When inhibition fails, one or both hands land on 10 and 11. Rouleau and colleagues called this a stimulus-bound response and found it more often in Alzheimer disease than in Huntington disease.

The same time also places both hands in the upper half of the face, so a left-sided spatial omission is easy to see, and it requires two hands of different lengths. Equal-length hands are a separate error from a wrong time.

Command, then copy

The command condition is the unprompted drawing above. The copy condition is a second drawing made while a correct clock stays in view. Royall’s CLOX was built around that split: CLOX1, the unprompted clock, tracked an executive measure (the EXIT25), and CLOX2, the copy, tracked the Mini-Mental State Examination. In their sample the EXIT25 accounted for most of the variance in CLOX1.

The gap between the two drawings is the teaching point.

  • In Alzheimer disease, Rouleau’s group found a marked improvement from command to copy. The model supplies the concept the person could not generate. A later two-year follow-up of the same paradigm found a steeper decline on command than on copy, with conceptual errors present early.
  • In Huntington disease, the same comparison did not show that marked copy improvement. Graphic difficulty stayed on the page.
  • In Lewy body dementia, Gnanalingham and colleagues found the opposite of the Alzheimer pattern: controls, people with Alzheimer disease, and people with Parkinson disease all improved from the draw condition to the copy, and people with Lewy body dementia scored equally poorly on both.

Ask for the copy whenever the command clock is abnormal. A copy that cleans up points toward planning or conceptual failure. A copy that stays broken points toward visuoconstruction or motor execution.

What the drawing is asking the brain to do

Four jobs are packed into one figure. Knowing what a clock is, including that it has twelve hours and two hands, draws on semantic memory, with temporal and temporoparietal cortex doing much of that work. Placing marks around a circle draws on parietal visuospatial and constructional cortex. Choosing the 2 for “ten minutes,” stopping at 12, and resisting the pull toward the spoken numbers draws on frontal and frontostriatal executive control. Making the line at all draws on motor cortex, basal ganglia, and cerebellum. A tremor, chorea, paresis, or severe arthritis can spoil a clock whose idea was intact.

The Cognitive Domains section of this site places the clock under both executive functioning and motor construction, which is why the MoCA gives it 3 points and the SLUMS gives it 4. Those points are a screen inside a screen. The figures below are for pattern recognition after the score, when you want to know what kind of error you are looking at.

Scoring systems, briefly

Several published systems exist. They do not use the same time, the same points, or the same cutoff, and Shulman noted that despite that variability the published psychometric properties looked broadly similar. Learn the one your clinic uses, and name it in the note.

  • Shulman (1986, reviewed 2000). A short ordinal scale, commonly used as 0–5, that emphasizes the qualitative look of the drawing: a perfect clock at the top, severe visuospatial disorganization or no recognizable clock at the bottom. The 2000 review is the best single orientation to the menu of systems.
  • Sunderland (1989). A 10 (best) to 1 (worst) scale. The requested time was 2:45. Anchors run from a correct clock down to drawings that are no longer recognizable as clocks. Useful as a severity correlate in a research or office setting where full neuropsychological testing is not available.
  • Mini-Cog (Borson, 2000). Three-item recall plus a clock, designed as a roughly three-minute screen. In the development sample — 129 people with probable dementia and 120 with no history of decline, 124 of them non-English speakers — sensitivity was 99% and 96% of subjects were correctly classified, and education and language did not move the result the way they moved the MMSE. The authors asked for validation in further clinical and population samples before treating those figures as settled. The clock here is one component of the screen, scored normal or abnormal, alongside recall.
  • CLOX (Royall, 1998). Two clocks. CLOX1 is the unprompted command and is the executive clock. CLOX2 is the copy. The difference is the point of the test.

What lowers a score for non-cognitive reasons. Ainslie and Murden asked non-demented older adults to draw a clock at 3:00 and scored it three ways. On the Shulman scale, sensitivity and specificity for dementia in the subgroup with 8 or fewer years of school were 90% and 42%. They concluded that educational effects make clock drawing a poor single screen in a poorly educated population. Limited experience with analog clocks, low literacy, uncorrected vision, and motor disease (tremor, paresis, arthritis, chorea) belong on that same list. Ask whether the person has used a clock face, can see the page, and can control the pen before you interpret the figure as cortex.

Clinical pearls

  • Give the three instructions one at a time. Pre-drawing the circle, or placing the 12 for the person, removes part of what you were trying to test.
  • Use 10 past 11 when you want the stimulus-bound error to be available. If your scale specifies another time, use that time.
  • When the command clock is abnormal, ask for a copy before you decide the error is spatial.
  • Write down the kind of error, not only a number: stimulus-bound hands, conceptual loss, left-sided omission, perseveration past 12, graphic distortion, or a clock that is poor on both command and copy.
  • A better-preserved clock in a frontal behavioral syndrome is expected often enough that a “good clock” does not argue against behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia. Blair’s frontotemporal group outperformed the Alzheimer group.
  • A clock that is poor on both command and copy, in a person with fluctuation, dream enactment, or parkinsonism, belongs in a Lewy-body workup. Gnanalingham described that copy failure. Cahn-Weiner’s classification rates, 69% and 68%, are why the clock stays a clue.
  • Palmqvist and colleagues offered a practical combination in MMSE-matched patients, not a clock-only rule. In the MMSE 21–27 range, an orientation subscore multiplied by 3 that was at least as high as the total MMSE, together with an impaired clock, had sensitivity 93% and specificity 70% for Lewy body dementia versus Alzheimer disease. Add a cube copy that is not three-dimensional and the question is stronger. Then use consensus criteria, not the clock.
  • In aphasia, rescore the possibility that the command was not understood. A word written in place of a numeral is language. An empty left half is spatial.
  • Record the scale you used. “Clock 3/5, Shulman” is interpretable later. “Clock abnormal” is not.
